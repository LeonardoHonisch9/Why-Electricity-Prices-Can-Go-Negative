# Why Electricity Prices Can Go Negative — Merit-Order Market Simulation

A small Python simulation of a day-ahead electricity market, built to accompany
the report *"Why Electricity Prices Can Go Negative: A Simplified Market
Simulation"*.

## How the market works

Each hour, generators submit an offer: how much power they can supply (MW) and
at what price (EUR/MWh). The market **sorts offers from cheapest to most
expensive** and accepts capacity in that order until demand is met. The price
offered by the last (marginal) accepted generator becomes the price paid to
**every** accepted generator for that hour — this is the merit-order / uniform
pricing rule used in real day-ahead power exchanges.

If enough near-zero or negative-cost generation (e.g. wind, which is cheaper
to run than to shut off) is available to cover all of demand by itself, the
marginal generator can itself have a negative offer price — and so can the
market price.

## Project structure

```
electricity_market/
    model.py       # Generator dataclass + clear_market() merit-order logic
    scenarios.py   # Builds 24h demand / wind / solar profiles
simulate.py         # Runs two day scenarios, prints hourly results, saves plots
heatmap_sweep.py     # Sweeps wind/solar capacity independently -- shows it's wind, not solar, that drives negative prices
threshold_sweep.py   # Scales wind+solar together vs. demand -- finds the linear negative-price threshold
app/index.html       # Interactive browser app — no build step, no server
plots/               # Output charts + sweep_results.csv (generated by the scripts above)
```

## Running it

```bash
pip install -r requirements.txt
python simulate.py
```

This prints an hour-by-hour clearing price table for two scenarios and saves
three charts to `plots/`:

- `typical_day.png` — a normal day: gas/nuclear set the price most hours.
- `negative_price_day.png` — a mild-demand day with strong wind and full sun:
  price goes negative for 11 of 24 hours, overnight and around midday, exactly
  when demand is lowest and wind/solar output is highest.
- `comparison.png` — both price curves on one chart.

## Customizing scenarios

`electricity_market/scenarios.build_day()` accepts overrides for demand base
level, demand swing, and wind/solar peak capacity, so you can try your own
"what if" scenarios, e.g.:

```python
from electricity_market.scenarios import build_day
from electricity_market.model import clear_market

demand, hourly_generators = build_day(demand_base=40, wind_peak=90)
for h in range(24):
    result = clear_market(hourly_generators[h], demand[h])
    print(h, result.market_price, result.marginal_generator)
```

## Interactive explorer

Open `app/index.html` in any browser (no server needed) to explore the model
yourself. Every generator's capacity **and offer price** is a slider —
including wind and solar's — so you can directly test questions like "what if
solar also bid below zero?" (there's a preset button for exactly that). You
get a live heatmap (negative-price hours vs. wind/solar capacity), a live
24-hour price chart, and a dynamic explanation of which generator's price is
currently capable of pushing the market negative. Good for showing on screen
in a video.

## Key finding: it's not "renewables" in general — it's who bids below zero

Sweeping wind and solar capacity independently (`heatmap_sweep.py`) shows the
heatmap forms **vertical bands**: the number of negative-price hours tracks
wind capacity almost exactly, and barely moves with solar capacity at all —
even though solar output is often larger in MW terms.

The reason is the offer price, not the technology: in this model wind bids
-10 EUR/MWh (it profits from a subsidy even at a loss on the energy itself)
while solar bids 0 EUR/MWh (it's cheap but not paid to run). Only a generator
bidding *below* zero can push the marginal price below zero. So the real
driver of negative prices is **any generator with an incentive to bid below
zero, sized larger than demand — renewables are today's example of that, not
the cause in principle.**

Lowering demand (`demand_base=50` vs `demand_base=80`) shifts the negative-
price threshold to a lower wind capacity and roughly doubles the worst-case
number of negative hours per day (8 → 17 in the two swept scenarios) —
confirming demand level and renewable capacity interact, not just each acting
alone.

## Second finding: it's the renewable-to-demand ratio, not either alone

`threshold_sweep.py` scales wind and solar together (holding their ratio
fixed) and sweeps that combined "renewable capacity multiplier" against
demand, finding the exact multiplier at which negative prices first appear
at each demand level (`plots/negative_price_threshold.png`,
`plots/negative_hours_heatmap.png`, raw data in `plots/sweep_results.csv`).

The threshold curve comes out **almost perfectly linear**: the minimum
renewable multiplier needed for even one negative-price hour scales
proportionally with demand (threshold ≈ demand ÷ 52 in this model's units).
In other words, what determines whether a grid sees negative prices isn't
the absolute size of its renewable fleet or its absolute demand — it's the
**ratio between the two**. A grid with twice the demand needs roughly twice
the renewable capacity before its first negative-price hour appears.

## Model limitations

## Where do the -10 and 0 €/MWh offer prices come from?

These match the report's worked example (Section 2.3), which doesn't cite a
source — they're a teaching example, not fitted to data. Checking them
against how real markets work:

- **Wind's -10 €/MWh is grounded in a real subsidy mechanism, and if
  anything conservative.** In the US, wind generators receive the federal
  Production Tax Credit (PTC), historically worth roughly $22-27/MWh after
  tax (about $34/MWh pre-tax). A wind generator still profits as long as the
  negative market price doesn't exceed the pre-tax value of the credit, so a
  wind producer can rationally bid around -$34/MWh and still break even.
  Real subsidy-driven wind bids go considerably deeper negative than -10.
  ([CRS report](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R42818),
  [Master Resource](https://www.masterresource.org/negative-pricing-wind/negative-wind-bid-ptc-2012-warning-texas/))

- **Solar's 0 €/MWh is a standard simplifying assumption in the bidding
  literature, not something intrinsic to the technology.** It reflects that
  a renewable producer with no marginal fuel cost can offer at price zero.
  Solar has no per-MWh production subsidy comparable to the US wind PTC, so
  there's less structural reason for it to bid below zero under that market
  design. But this depends entirely on policy design: under some European
  feed-in-tariff schemes, the grid operator guarantees settlement for
  subsidized renewable production (wind *and* solar) by bidding it in at
  extremely negative prices — one study models this at around
  -3000 €/MWh for both technologies.
  ([Revisiting the Merit-Order Effect of Renewable Energy Sources](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.0444))

**Takeaway:** whether a generator bids below zero is a policy/subsidy design
question, not a physical property of wind vs. solar. That's exactly why the
app lets you set solar's offer price below zero — under a different (equally
realistic) subsidy regime, it would be.

This is a deliberately simplified single-node model. It does not include:
- Transmission/network constraints or congestion
- Ramp rates or minimum stable generation levels for conventional plants
- Forecast error (offers are treated as perfectly known in advance)
- Demand response (demand is perfectly inelastic — a vertical line)
- Strategic bidding or market power

These are natural extensions if you want to take the model further.
